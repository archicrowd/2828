The Golden Third

A static costing and distributional model of a shared tax and social-protection framework for a group of European states

Initiative 28:28

Model version v4.81 · 1 November 2026 · Licence CC BY 4.0 · Research kit and figures to cite: doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23855733 · Website: archicrowd.github.io/goldenes-drittel/ · Contact: tax.better@archicrowd.de

Abstract

We describe a proposal under which a group of states adopts common rules for income taxation and social protection, and we document a transparent, static model of its costs and distributional effects. The proposal pursues four objectives: tax fairness across labour, capital and automation; fairness between generations; coping with climate change; and coping with the digital transformation of work. Income of every kind is taxed in steps of 0, 11, 19, 25 and 33 per cent tied to each country's basic need; social contributions are abolished and employers must pass at least 80 per cent of the employer contributions that disappear into wages; a tax-financed safety net covers health care, long-term care and basic needs; every adult receives a monthly transformation dividend; 10 per cent of gross wages go into a personal, locked pension account; and public debt and unfunded pension promises are moved into a common fund repaid by 2098. For 2028, in purchasing power of 2026, a single employee at the average wage gains between 281 € and 726 € per month in Germany, France, Italy and Spain and 1,398 zł in Poland. The Gini coefficient falls in all five countries, by 6.2 points at most. The common fund requires 5.15 per cent of GDP a year, a surcharge of 15.4 per cent on all taxes at an interest rate of 2.45 per cent, and peaks at 147.5 per cent of GDP in 2055. Losers are German pensioner households (only 64.5 per cent better off), single earners in France at half the average wage, and one-earner couples at twice the average wage. The model has no behavioural responses; the main uncertainties are the pass-through of employer contributions, the fund interest rate and the tax base of company profits taxed where customers are. Measured against its objectives, the model supports the claims on distribution and on the robustness of revenue to a shift from labour to capital income; it shows tensions between climate success and the size of the dividend, and between legacy repayment and today's pensioners; and it cannot yet show effects on emissions.

Keywords: tax reform, social protection, basic income, transformation dividend, payroll tax incidence, public debt, Europe. JEL: H20, H24, H55, H63, I38.

1 Introduction

Most European states finance social protection to a large extent through contributions levied on wages, combine them with progressive income taxes and an array of means-tested benefits, and carry public debt and unfunded pension promises from the past. The resulting systems are complex, and the marginal burden on additional earnings is high in parts of the income distribution. The proposal examined here – named The Golden Third after its upper tax rate of one third – replaces this structure with a small number of common rules that a group of states could adopt jointly.

This paper does not derive an optimal tax system. It is a costing and distributional accounting exercise: given the rules, what does the system cost, who gains and who loses compared with the law of 2026, and how sensitive are these results to the main assumptions? All figures come from one calculation chain of 77 stages based on official statistics. Every change to the model is recorded in a log, and every error found is numbered as a correction (86 so far). The chain was recalculated in three internal reviews, carried out with a different AI model from the one used to build the calculation; the last review covered model version v4.42 (section 10).

Section 2 states the objectives, section 3 the rules, section 4 the model and data, section 5 the results, section 6 the sensitivity to key assumptions. Section 7 assesses the results against the objectives, including the tensions between them. Section 8 summarises behavioural evidence that the static model does not capture, section 9 limitations and open questions, section 10 the review history.

2 Objectives

The initiative states four objectives. They are normative choices of the initiative; this paper does not argue for them but asks whether the rules are consistent with them and what the model can show.

Tax fairness. Today labour carries most of the burden: in Germany the tax wedge on an average wage is about 49.3 % of labour costs (OECD 2026), while capital income is taxed at a flat rate of about 26 %. Social contributions fall on human work only, so replacing work by machines saves contributions. The proposal treats labour, capital and automation alike: no income is taxed at more than one third, without deductions, and company profits – explicitly including digital platforms – are taxed where the customers are, because profits can be shifted but customers cannot. Fairness is also vertical: a floor at basic need and a dividend for everyone.

Intergenerational fairness. Public debt and unfunded pension promises are commitments made by earlier generations that today's systems pass on to later ones. The proposal makes them explicit, moves them into a common fund and repays them by 2098. Pension promises above basic need are reduced, so that today's pensioners carry a share; basic need is guaranteed. New saving for old age is funded on an own account rather than promised by later contributors. The dividend is justified as a share for everyone in what earlier generations built – knowledge, technology, infrastructure.

Climate change. The proposal relies on economic incentives rather than on control of individual products and firms. A carbon price and a special VAT rate of 33 % on harmful goods make harmful consumption more expensive; their revenue is returned to everyone through the dividend, so that households that consume little gain. VAT becomes a label with five classes. Suppliers move their products into lower classes by certification at approved bodies under state supervision: the state supervises the certifiers, not each product. The intended result is a competition for sustainability in which a lower rate is a price advantage that suppliers earn. The dividend shrinks as emissions fall; the initiative regards this as intended.

Digital transformation of work. If more output is produced by machines and software and less by paid work, systems financed from wages lose their base and tie protection to employment that becomes less stable. The proposal finances social protection from all income, makes the net and the dividend independent of employment, keeps the pension account portable across jobs, and taxes platforms where their customers are. The dividend is meant to give everyone a share of what automation and digitalisation bring in.

3 The proposal

Income taxRates 0 / 11 / 19 / 25 / 33 % in brackets defined as multiples of each country's basic need. Gross income without deductions; individual taxation (no splitting or family quotient). Capital income, profits and inheritance are taxed at most at 33 %; company profits are taxed where customers are, including digital platforms.
ContributionsSocial contributions are abolished. Employers are legally obliged to pass at least 80 % of the employer contributions to social security that disappear into gross wages, up to six times basic need; a fallback rule applies if contracts are not adjusted by the start date.
Strong netHealth care, long-term care (partial), minimum pension, basic needs in disability and unemployment, financed from general taxes. A floor equal to basic need times an equivalence scale (0.5 for each further adult, 0.55 per child) is guaranteed; own earnings count by half. In Germany the floor is 1,029 € per month for a single person (2028, purchasing power 2026). Low earners are topped up on an application that the authority pre-fills.
DividendMonthly payment to every adult, 80 % for children, without application; financed from steering taxes (carbon and other) and from gains of digitalisation. It falls as emissions fall.
Own routeCompulsory saving of 10 % of gross wages into a personal account, locked until retirement age, with few investment options and a default fund.
VAT labelRates 5 / 11 / 17 / 25 %, special rate 33 % only for harmful goods. Suppliers obtain lower rates through certification by supervised bodies.
LegacyPublic debt and unfunded pension promises above the dividend move into a common fund of the participating states, financed by a surcharge on all taxes. Existing pension promises are protected up to an allowance of twice basic need after 35 insurance years (pro rata for shorter careers, never below basic need) and reduced in steps above it (27.5 %, then 55.1 %); own savings are not reduced (pension rule BS35). The effective reduction of pension payments differs by state, from 0.5 % to 29.6 % (6.95 % in Germany).

4 Model and data

Scope. The calculations cover a possibility space of 30 European states with 465.6 million inhabitants and a GDP of 19,515 billion euro (2024). Ten states are calculated in detail; the common fund of 30 states is the decision of the ten scaled by the ratio 30/10 from the possibility space (assumption). Household results are computed for five reference countries: Germany, France, Italy, Spain and Poland.

Revenue and expenditure. Tax bases (wages, profits, consumption, wealth, inheritance) and current expenditure come from Eurostat national and government accounts (gov_10a_exp, gov_10a_taxag, nama_10_gdp); unfunded pension claims from the supplementary pension tables (nasa_10_pens1, table 29); debt from gov_10dd_edpt1. The tax base of company profits taxed where customers are is the largest position that is not yet documented against an independent source.

Households today. Each reference country has a simplified model of 2026 law: income tax, employee and employer contributions and cash benefits including minimum income schemes (Germany: Bürgergeld approximation with housing and child supplements; France: APL and prime d'activité; Italy: Assegno di Inclusione; Spain: Ingreso Mínimo Vital and child supplement; Poland: zasiłek okresowy). Parameters come partly from official sources and partly from secondary sources; the parameter files mark each block as official, secondary or assumption. The models were checked against the OECD tax wedge: 7 of 10 household types lie within ±2 percentage points (Table 3).

Germany. A synthetic microsimulation, calibrated to official totals, covers employees, civil servants, self-employed, minimum-income recipients and pensioners. Validation against 2025: gross wages −2.8 %, wage tax −5.4 %. Pensioner incomes come from EU-SILC 2025 and the German old-age income survey (ASID 2023), both as published aggregates. No micro data are used.

Distribution. The Gini coefficient for 2028 is the official value for 2025 (EU-SILC) plus the change computed on the published decile distribution. VAT burdens by income come from the German household budget survey (EVS 2023) and, for France, from Eurostat's Household Budget Survey 2020; Italy, Spain and Poland use a factor of 1 so far.

Conventions. Amounts for 2028 are in purchasing power of 2026. The model is static: labour supply, saving, prices beyond a one-off level effect and migration do not respond. Pass-through of employer contributions, take-up, avoidance and VAT incidence enter as parameters or bands. Take-up of the floor is assumed to be complete.

Three patterns stand out. 5.2 Germany: all households and pensioners

In the German microsimulation 85.1 % of working-age households and 78.6 % of all households are better off. By status of the main earner: full-time 99.1 %, part-time 99.9 %, self-employed 77.7 %, civil servants 56.4 %, mini-job 49.1 %, households without earnings 18.0 %. Under pension rule BS35, 64.5 % of pensioner households are better off; the mean change is −116 € per month, from +30 € in the lowest to −599 € in the highest fifth. The rule asks the contribution to the legacy fund mainly from promises above twice basic need; long careers with modest promises keep their pension in full and gain from the removal of health and care contributions. Insurance years are imputed from the pension amount (assumption), as the survey data do not record them.

6 Sensitivity

Pass-through of employer contributions. This is the most important single assumption. Evidence from Sweden shows that employers did not pass a cut in payroll taxes for young workers into their wages individually (Saez, Schoefer and Seim 2019); the proposal therefore makes pass-through a legal obligation with a fallback rule. Without any pass-through, the German single at € 42,000 still gains +258 €, but in France only 38 % and in Spain 53 % of single employees would be better off (Table 8).

Avoidance at the top. Revenue from inheritance (1.16 % of GDP) and large wealth (0.97 % of GDP after avoidance) depends on behaviour at the top of the distribution, where responses are strong (Jakobsen et al. 2020; Kleven et al. 2014). Across the avoidance band the German dividend changes by −55 to +25 € per adult and month in 2028.

Take-up. The model assumes complete take-up of the floor. Today, non-take-up of minimum income is high in Germany and France (DREES 2022; Eurofound 2015); a payment without application and a pre-filled top-up are designed to reduce it, which raises costs that are already fully counted.

Tax fairness. Inequality of disposable income falls in all five countries. The burden on labour falls and the burden on capital rises, which is the intended shift. The marginal burden on high wages also falls, however: at € 100,000 from 53.1 % to 39.1 %, at € 250,000 from 44.3 % to 40.0 % (Table 7). The proposal thus gives up part of the progressivity on high wages in exchange for equal treatment of income types; whether this is fair depends on whether top incomes actually pay the 33 % on capital income, inheritance and profits, which the model treats with an avoidance band (section 6). One-earner couples lose the advantage of joint taxation; this is consistent with individual taxation but is a loss for these households.

Intergenerational fairness. Repayment by 2098 means that taxpayers after 2098 no longer carry today's legacy. The cost falls on taxpayers until then through the surcharge, and on pensioners with promises above basic need: German pensioner households lose 116 € per month on average, while the lowest fifth gains slightly. A full assessment would need generational accounts by birth cohort, which the model does not compute.

Climate: incentive instead of control. The rules are consistent with the objective in that harmful consumption is taxed and the revenue returned. Three elements work through prices rather than prohibitions:

  • Carbon price. The European carbon price for buildings and transport (ETS2) enters as a revenue path; its revenue falls with emissions even though the price rises. Road transport fuel expenditure follows the German projection report 2026 (with-measures scenario).
  • Special rate on harmful goods. Goods classified as harmful to health or the environment carry 33 % – in the model motor fuels, combustion-engine vehicles, tobacco and alcohol; chemical pesticides and mineral fertilisers are included by decision of 4 November 2026 (not yet in the calculation; the household share of these purchases is small). Because firms deduct VAT as input tax, the special rate does not reach agricultural use of pesticides and fertilisers; a levy differentiated by harmfulness, as in Denmark, is an open proposal. The model assumes that the share of combustion engines in vehicle purchases falls from 80 % in 2028 to 10 % in 2035 (assumption, oriented to EU vehicle CO₂ standards).
  • VAT label. Table 13 shows the price signal implied by the rates alone. For the same net price, a certified product in class A costs 5.1 % less than a standard product; a harmful product costs 13.7 % more. A supplier who keeps the consumer price unchanged earns 5.4 % more per unit after moving from B to A. Certification pays if its cost is lower than this advantage.

Which share of a rate difference reaches consumers or stays with suppliers depends on pass-through, which is asymmetric for VAT changes (Benzarti et al. 2020). Assignment of goods to classes is set by law; lower classes A+ and A are reached through certification.

The label is distributionally progressive in its burden (Table 6), and the revenue returns per head through the dividend. Evidence supports the direction of the mechanism but not its size: consumers respond more strongly to taxes shown at the point of purchase (Chetty, Looney and Kroft 2009); in a systematic review of environmental labels on food, 60 of 76 comparisons favoured the labelled option, with many studies at unclear or high risk of bias (Potter et al. 2021). Certification works only if certifiers are independent and supervised (Dranove and Jin 2010). VAT cuts are passed on to consumers less than increases (Benzarti et al. 2020), so part of the advantage of class A may stay with suppliers – which still rewards certification, but weakens the price signal to consumers. Carbon rebates are often underestimated by recipients (Mildenberger et al. 2022).

The model does not estimate how far the rules reduce emissions: emissions are an exogenous path, and the demand response to the label is not quantified for the current rates. The legal frame is open. Current EU law allows at most two reduced rates of at least 5 % and one lower rate (Council Directive (EU) 2022/542), while the label has three rates below the standard class; rates that depend on a supplier's certification rather than on the product raise the question of fiscal neutrality (CJEU case C-515/20; secondary source). The same directive already points in the direction of the label: it permits reduced rates for solar panels and ends reduced rates for fossil fuels from 2030.

The success of climate policy reduces steering revenue from 3.14 % of GDP in 2028 to 0.67 % in 2060 and is the main reason why the dividend falls. Later cohorts therefore receive a smaller dividend – a tension with intergenerational fairness that the initiative accepts as intended.

Digital transformation of work. The clearest quantitative result concerns the tax base. Today a shift of three points of GDP from labour to capital income by 2060 would reduce revenue by 0.64 % of GDP, because capital income is taxed more lightly; in the new system the same shift raises revenue by 0.33 % of GDP. Faster productivity growth from automation raises the dividend in all scenarios (Table 12). Protection no longer depends on a job. Whether displacement by automation is large, and how labour markets adjust, is outside the model; the scenarios are assumptions.

8 Behavioural effects not in the model

A qualitative review of the literature (available in German in the research kit) assessed the expected responses to each building block:

  • Dividend: experimental and quasi-experimental evidence finds small reductions in hours and little reduction in employment (Jones and Marinescu 2022; Vivalt et al. 2024; STM 2020).
  • Payment without application: lowers non-take-up (Bhargava and Manoli 2015; Eurofound 2015); costs are already fully counted.
  • Compulsory saving with a default fund: raises saving of passive savers, with partial crowd-out among active savers (Madrian and Shea 2001; Chetty et al. 2014).
  • Individual taxation: raises hours of second earners (Bick and Fuchs-Schündeln 2018).
  • VAT label: cuts reach consumers less than increases (Benzarti et al. 2020); consumers respond more strongly to taxes that are visible at the point of purchase (Chetty, Looney and Kroft 2009), which may strengthen the steering effect of a label (not tested).
  • Carbon price with rebate: rebates are underestimated and do not reliably raise support (Douenne and Fabre 2022; Mildenberger et al. 2022).
  • Pension rule BS35: loss aversion among pensioners with high promises (marginal cut 55.1 %) is an acceptance and legal risk not captured by the model.

None of these findings questions the calculation as such; several show that outcomes depend on design details that are not yet fixed. A quantitative stage is open.

9 Limitations and open questions

  • The model is static; behavioural responses are discussed but not quantified.
  • Country models are simplified: one earner per household; regional rules and housing benefits beyond those named are not modelled; several parameters rest on secondary sources.
  • The share of employees better off uses the German wage distribution scaled to each country's average wage; national wage distributions and two-earner couples are open.
  • VAT burdens by income are available only for Germany and France. The demand response to the label and the effect of the rules on emissions are not quantified; the label requires changes to EU VAT law.
  • The tax base of company profits taxed where customers are has not been checked against an independent source.
  • The common fund of 30 states is scaled from ten states; interest and growth are assumptions with a wide band.
  • Compatibility with EU law and national constitutions has not been assessed.
  • Which states could adopt common rules is open. Broad acceptance and reliable enforcement are preconditions; the World Justice Project's Rule of Law Index (2025) is one possible criterion. States outside Europe are not calculated.

10 Reviews, corrections and access

The calculation chain was recalculated in three internal reviews in October 2026, initiated by the initiative and carried out with a different AI model from the one used to build the calculation; they are not independent reviews. The second and third reviews re-implemented the core in their own code and confirmed it at rounding level; their findings and the responses are documented. The last review covered model version v4.42; the changes since then (up to v4.81) are listed on the website; a follow-up review of them is under way, in which the fund grid (D1) and the marginal burden (D2) have been reproduced in full so far. Independent review is invited.

The log records every version with date, change, data source, moved figure and how it was noticed; 86 corrections have been numbered so far. The research kit with all figures to cite, the result files and the review documents is archived at doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23855733. Scripts, parameters and the full log are public at github.com/archicrowd/2828.
